Just who is your enemy?
In light of recent interactions that I've had with some people, I think that it would be in the best interest of both myself and everyone else (more on that later) to clarify who I consider an enemy--and why.

First and foremost, of course, most people--or at least Americans--will answer this question quite firmly: Islamic Fundamentalists. To the surprise of some, I'll agree with that, but probably for different reasons. Islamic Fundamentalists wish to impose a strict medieval code of conduct upon anyone within their own jurisdiction--and beyond, if they can--and as such they are enemies of the common man and woman. A religious dictatorship, as anyone who has studied history even slightly should know by now, is the worst dictatorship of all. Any action can be justified by the consent of a God that cannot truly give consent. And yet, most working-class Americans (and the general "western" working class) have forgotten their historical enemies. Police, soldiers, factory workers, clerks, waiters and waitresses, whatever the job--we've all somehow forgotten who it is that controls our lives, our families and our ability to pursue happiness. I'm speaking, of course, of the Bourgeoisie--but why use that word? Few people know what it really means, and even fewer care to learn.

Instead, I'm going to say that the rich are the enemy--although not all rich people are part of the Bourgeoisie. Most Americans and "Westerners", (an apt term that I'll use for the duration of this post) have forgotten in the past century that our lives are subject to the whims of the rich. We don't have a choice to work for them--we either work for them, or we don't work; which means we don't eat, play or love--or live for very long. A handful of people own the vast majority of all property in the United States, as well as in the rest of the world, and the number of them has gotten smaller over time. Ironically, or maybe not so, what they own and the value of it has increased incredulously. Some people will, undoubtedly, claim that the rich are rich because they worked to be rich--but this is rarely the case. Most of the rich in "the west" are the heirs of families that, historically, have ruled the countries since their conception--and sometimes before that. And on the rare occassion that a poor person rises up the social ladder, his interests change. The rich are concerned with protecting the rich, by whatever means necessary, and this includes keeping the poor.. well, poor, but entertained. In Rome, the rich openly admitted this: provide the population with bread and circuses and they'll happily let themselves be exploited, even to die in wars that seem just. Today, replace bread with a Big Mac and fries and replace the circus with yet another music album, television series or scandal and you have the same situation--without, of course, today's elite rulers admitting it. The rich, over the past thousands of years, have learned to be less open about the power they wield.

One of the biggest farces in the history of humanity is that of the United States Government and the governments that modelled themselves after its democratic ways. This isn't to say that anyone under jurisdiction of the US is part of it, but that this government hides a much more insidious form of rule than it seems. Every major politician for the past two centuries has been in the pocket of the rich. Money, in the states and many other places, counts as votes after it's all said and done. You can vote for anyone you want, but it's the rich who have the final decision. With very little research, you'll be able to find that there isn't a single politician in high offices that isn't worth millions. As such, almost everything a politician does is directly in the interest of the rich, although sometimes those things can benefit the working person as a side effect. Wars are declared by the rich, for the rich; laws are created to keep the rich in power and to keep the workers working for the rich. When we go to work, we aren't working for ourselves; we are working for the profit of someone that already owns half of the world. Soldiers and police, while I'm sure that most of them enter into service to protect their own families, friends and the common man, often die in the service of the rich--to keep them rich and make them richer. Soldiers, by and large, are victims of a system that doesn't care about the welfare of the common man or woman, but pretends to do so.

What few people realize is that the philosophies of Karl Marx, undiluted by the horrendous affairs of the Soviet leadership that happened without his input or consent, stem directly from the same fruits that founded the United States and the modern Europe we see today. While many communists have disagreements with the founding fathers of the United States--slavery, of course, one of the many things we find distasteful--many of us have an admiration for many of the ideas the United States originally stood for precisely because it is part of our own philosophy. Both Karl Marx and Thomas Jefferson used the same system of philosophy to justify their own positions, and in many cases their positions were the same--the difference, of course, being that Marx came a century later and built upon the philosophy of his time, including that of Jefferson. The major tenants of these philosophies--that is, marxism and Jeffersonian democracy--are: fraternity, equality and liberty. These are the things that Communists are supposed to stand for, and these are the things that the United States are supposed to stand for.

Unfortunately, neither have turned out to actually put those things into practice. Communism, as most anyone knows, is almost a dirty word in the United States and most of the western countires throughout, and with some good reasons: the Soviet Union surely did not accomplish the major goals of fraternity, equality or liberty in any sense (avoiding the was it communist, was it not? argument). Yet many people forget that the United States has not stood for that philosophy for quite some time, either. I might confess that the United States does have a sense of fraternity, in that the population generally believes itself to be unified in a common cause, but it has strayed far from the philosophy of liberty and equality and with it so have many other countires that moulded themselves in the 'States image.

We may have more "freedoms" here in the "West" than, say, Sudan--but is it even fair to compare the two? Should we wholly support a government of the rich, by the rich simply because we're better off than the worst of the worst? I think not. "Some freedoms" is not freedom, and "some equality" is not equality. If people wish to stand for the basic tenants of fraternity, equality and liberty, then it is the duty of each and every one of us to act against a government that does not live up to those standards.

The biggest difference that Libertarian Communists such as myself (despite disliking all the classifications) have with most other working-class is the question of economy. Communists believe that nobody should starve. Communists believe that nobody should be forced to work solely for the profit of another, most especially not for the profit of someone that is already in control of our lives. Communists believe that the work of one should be the work of all; that is, we're all in this together. We should work for the betterment of all of us, not for the betterment of a select few rich people who in turn kill us in wars, destroy the air we breath and limit our freedoms. We should live and work free and equal, not sometimes free and even less equal.

I do feel, however, that I need to put a disclaimer here. I don't think that all communists are good communists, just as I don't believe all people are good people. For those of you that turn blue at the mere mention of communists, keep in mind that I am no more responsible for the atrocities of the Soviet Union than a Christian is for the atrocities of the Catholic church, or any "white man" is for slavery or an American for the destruction of the native population in the Americas. These things happen in the name of us, but they do not represent us. I would no more blame a random American for the atrocities done in their name in the past than I would blame myself for Stalin. In sum, I hope that this clarifies my position a bit despite not posting here often now, and hopefully the position of those not so articulate but who feel the same, to those of you that honestly don't know the basic tenants that communists hold dear: fraternity, equality and liberty. Most of us, communist and non-communist alike, can agree that these are the ideals that should always be worked towards. The question is, since we aren't there now, how do we get there? That's what communists ask, and the answer is still elusive.

But we must ask the question, we must try to find the answer, and we must do our best to put those ideals in practice. Our lives, our children and our own liberation depend on it. I'm going to end this with a quote of Thomas Jefferson, in the hope that some people will find my reasoning in their own values - communist or not:

"A society that will trade a little liberty for a little order will lose both, and deserve neither"
