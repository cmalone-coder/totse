God and Perfection
Ok, this may have been brought up before, sorry if it has, but anyway... If God is perfect, as many religions claim, then there is a problem. If something is perfect, then it cannot change, else it becomes less perfect. In the first place, this means that there is something that anything can do but that God cannot, which would be wrong according to the descriptions of these religions- "God can do anything we can, and then some". Secondly, if God is perfect (and hence can't change) He's either not omniscient (ie. he cant know everything, because things are changing and so he would have to change with them which, according to the definition of perfection, is impossible), or he MUST exist outside of time so that nothing changes- it all happens at once, so he doesnt have to change to accomodate new things. This is also a limiting factor - God cannot exist in time, only in space, meaning again there is something else we can do that He cant. Either that or fatalism is true, and God knew what was happening from the start. But then, if God changed something at the start (eg made the world) he too would have to change to know that the world now exists(he may have known that it was GOING to exist, but that knowledge would have to change to the world NOW exists), yet another paradox. Any thoughts?

Re: God and Perfection
Ok, I'm jumping back into the fray!
First of all, I use 'He' for simplicity. I'm not going to say he/she/it/whatever everytime, and he is the standard proverb (and I personally see 'it' as inappropriate). Anyway, I'm not here to quibble about gender.
Secondly, tradition clearly states that God is perfect. I take this perfect as an objective thing - there is no way any objective being could calim God is not perfect. He (I'm sticking with it!!) is the dictionary definition of perfect if you will(no, I'm not going to quote the OED for you. You want the definition of perfect, look it up yourself!). I don't care about subjective perfection - that will, naturally, vary. I'm talking on the level of absolutes. There is an absolute for perfect, and tradition states that that thing is God. Perhaps another way of expressing it would be "posesses all perfections", which amounts to the same thing, but is a different way of expressing it.

Two new asides, now - one directly related, one kind of off track. If there is an 'outside of time' as I suggest as a cop-out means of getting around my argument, then this implies four-dimensionalism, which makes time travel possible... interesting, because whole new realms open up in arguments about fatalism, which can lead to a circular or paradoxical argument... not here, people!!
Secondly, if God 'posseses all perfections', isn't there an inherent contradiction there? For example, God is traditionally perfectly Just and perfectly Forgiving. Ignoring Just, for a second, what need have we for hell, or sins, or morals, if God is going to forgive us and let us into heaven anyway? And then, combine Forgiving with Just, a paradox arises - if one is perfectly Just, one metes out punishment as deserved for a crime (or sin or whatever). Yet if one is perfectly forgiving, then you do just that - forgive everything. The two are mutually exclusive. Also, if God is perfectly forgiving, then why the hell aren't we living in the Garden of Eden - a perfectly forgiving God would have never kicked Adam and Eve out for preaking his rules.

Responses to arguments raised: Already covered the definition of perfect and the subjectivity problem. I agree that "God preceeds logic" is a ridiculous argument. God is as subject to logic as we are. Is it logically possible for an omnipotent God to make a rock that he cannot lift? Think about it. If he can make such a rock, then he cant do something - lift it. If he can't make such a rock, there is something He can't do - make it. Either way he is not omnipotent unless we argue from logical possibility. And as to the idea that 'sameness' can envelope change, does anyone else see a glaring contradiction here? In no world can 'same' = 'different'.

Sorry about the length, but I think it's warranted. Anyway, I'm going to write an essay on this one of these days, so keep arguing with me!
