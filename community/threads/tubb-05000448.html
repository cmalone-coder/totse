Fair enough let mme throw this idea at you and tell me what you think then...

What I'm talking about is an extruded ceramic honeycomb structure of a refractory material such as cordierite or mullite, Formed metallic foil monolithic structures are also commonly used. This monolithic structure is wash coated with a thin layer of a catalyst carrier such as alumina or zirconium oxide of very high surface area. The high surface area carrier is usually impregnated uniformly throughout with a noble metal mixture such as platinum, palladium, rhodium.

Cordierite, a thermally rugged ceramic composed of magnesium oxide, silica, and alumina, is the most commonly used substrate material. Manufacturers use an extrusion method to form numerous long, narrow channels in the substrates--giving the bricks a honeycomb appearance. The substrates are designed to provide high surface area for catalytic reactions and to withstand rapid temperature fluctuations.

Catalyst companies turn such substrate bricks into catalytic converters by coating them with a mixture of precious metals and other materials in a roughly 25- to 50-mm-thick layer known as a washcoat. Thermally resistant g-alumina tends to be used as the base into which catalyst metals, stabilizers, and additives are dispersed. Some manufacturers use double washcoats to enhance certain types of reactivity and to improve durability by separating catalyst components.

Platinum, rhodium, and palladium have made their way into catalyst formulations in varying concentrations since the 1970s. Earlier models used more platinum than today's units, which are nearly all palladium, but in general, the amount of precious metal in the active catalyst has remained in the 0.10 to 0.15\ range.

You can search the internet a hundreds times over and you'll find no mention of any experimenters even exploring the possibilities of this ready made easily accessible catalyst.

Yet you'll find numerous reactions that rely upon platinum and palladium catalyst.

One potential experiment or theorectical use involving a tube furnace and salvaged auto catalyst would be the preparation of methylamine.

Nitromethane would be dripped down onto a vertical bed of said catalyst whole the system is purged with a steady flow of electrolytic hydrogen. The vapors travel through a condeser at the bottom of the furnace where they are cooled before being bubbled through a flask of hydrochloric acid.

The hydrogen reacts with the nitromethane over the catalyst to form methylamine & water
The condenser cools the hot vapors and the methylamine solution drips into the Hcl where it forms methylamine hydrochloride. The flask of acid also prevents any air from backing up into the hydrogen purge stream.

The methylamine hydrochloride will crystalize out when the water and unreacted nitromethane are evaporated off.
