Re: Isn't human evolution dead?
Well, I suppose evolution is somewhat vague in that it can be analyzed on a number of hierarchical levels (i.e. nucleotide, gene, morpholigical feature, gene pool composition...). But I think when you define the parameters correctly, it's actually quite precise.

So in terms of whether or not humans are still evolving, I think the general consensus is that we definitely are. In fact, it would essentially be impossible for any species to stop evolving - as that would require the gene pool to remain identical from generation to generation.

Something that certainly has changed in human evolution, however, is the activity of natural selection. That is, the selective forces that drive the direction of human evolution are becoming increasingly non-natural - i.e. dependent on socioeconomic, political, religious factors. It's definitely interesting though, because when it comes to natural selection and survival of the fittest there are only two things that matter: survival AND reproduction. At this point, we generally see weathly, well-educated people as being pretty 'fit'. However, people in this demographic tend to only have one or two children, who then go on to an ivy league school, get rich, and themselves have one or two children. In the long run this is a pretty paltry contribution to the gene pool, when you consider the much higher numbers of children borne by poorer, less-educated people. (I hope I'm not appearing to be classist or anything here. I'm speaking on really, really broad terms. And besides, I'm only moderately educated, and certainly not wealthy, so I couldn't be elitist even if I tried). Anyways, my point is that when you consider that the evolutionary game is won by those who contribute the most genes to the next generation, then it looks like there's irony in that fact that the people who appear the most fit are actually treading in the shallow end of the gene pool.

Re: Isn't human evolution dead?
Well, assuming we all agree on the theory of evolution, all life started as simple quasi-cellular replicators. They floated through the primordial sludge and voraciously created copies. Eventually the sequence of nitrogen bases in the nucleic acids was altered (whether by radiation or a simple maltranscription) either way, this change somehow made that particular replicator more efficient, and thus its copies were spread more rapidly. This process continued for billions of years, and eventually resulted in mammals including humans.
Now with that background established, one can attempt to apply it to modern human evolution. Any such attempt reveals an interesting dilemma, the insurrection of a new replicator. This new entity is not however an extension of DNA, but a new function entirely. It is in fact the meme, or thought. In human society, ones survival is increasingly dependent on the culture in which he takes part. (this culture can be a group of friends or an entire religion. All in all, this mental replicator has taken the role of dictating human survival. The Roman culture was ill-adapted, so they were obliterated, as with the Samurai.
How will this factor affect evolution in the next million years, we can only guess.
