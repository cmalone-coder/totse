It really doesn't matter WHAT you plug in beforehand, as you're still stuck by the limitations of the plug. Your bandwidth is only as big as the narrowest pipeline.

Mainly, I use MIDI over USB, just because it's easy, plug n play, and it can easially handle all the MIDI data that comes in. I've heard recording with FireWire isn't bad, but I've never done it personally.

When I do vocals, it goes from the mic, into the tube preamp (with warmth and a limiter), into a vocal processor, and out into the soundcard. Then I just take the raw recording, and gate it, add delay, chorus, etc.

Originally posted by heisler2:

Hoo boy.. it can get really complicated, but I'll try to explain the basics. This is assuming you have some knowledge of waveforms and/or sine waves specifically.


Two waves entirely (180 degrees) out of phase cancel each other out, resulting in no sound. In basic synthesis, to create other wave forms (triangles, squares, saws, etc), other waves are layed on a basic sine wave to modify it. These waves are layed at various frequencies and times, to form the wave. This relates to acoustics as well, hence why you'll get weird sorts of phasing depending on the environment your music is played in.

For instance, if you take a smaller since wave of higher hertz, and overlay it, you will begin to square out the first wave. Here's a picture. Ignore the formulas. I'll explain the image: The column on the left is what wave is added, the column on the right is the result. As the waves hit each other, they begin to cancel each other out (similar to phasing a track). However, since these waves aren't exactly 180 out of phase, they dont cancel each other.. instead, they modify the wave. By overlaying smaller, faster waves to a sine wave, it shapes the sine wave into a square shape, resulting in a square wave. Of course, a square wave actually consists of N different waves being used to square out the signal, not just 3.

That's your basic wave theory. Using that, you can make saws, triangles, square waves, and your usual sine waves. Most synthesisers have these as voltage controlled presets, so it's not entirely neccessary to know about them in synthesis, but it always helps to know, especially in referance to acoustics. The best example is a phaser, when it slightly misaligns the waveforms, resulting in a weird, "tube" sound.

First off, a softsynth? I've always referred to a SoftSynth as like a single synth, or a VST/DXi plugin (Sytrus, Maelstrom, Blockfish, Rapture, etc).

If you're talking about like production software (I know there's a better name that I'm missing), then I've got a few suggestions.

Over FL vs Reason, I'd take Reason anyday. It's far more powerful, but on the downside, it is far harder to use, especially for beginners. But for IDM specifically?


Project from FL 6: http://mindsdesire.net/kai/tzachitx.mp3

Project from Reason 3: http://mindsdesire.net/kai/music/SGX%20-%20Kick%20Your%20A.mp3


Personally though, I like Cakewalk Project 5 (although I'm still getting the hang of it, it's alot different than Reason or FL), because it supports VST/DXi plugins (like Cakewalk Rapture, which owns). So, that's my software of choice. I mainly run that, Reason, Live, and sometimes ACID if I need to work with samples. I've got a LOT more software, like Cubase, Sonar, and Audition, but I never seem to use it.

If you want to play a single WAV, just load the sample into the NN19 or NNXT.

The thing with those samplers are, is that they're made to go across the whole spectrum.. like a sample of a piano or something. You just need to load your sample into one note (like middle C), and then to fire it off, just press that key.
