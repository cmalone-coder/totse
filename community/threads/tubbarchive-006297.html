You will gain a LOT of general endurance with rope-skipping. It of course trains your legs very well, every single muscle in your legs is trained well by doing rope-skipping, and it is also very good for your back and stomach muscles. The strenghth of your legs will become far more than average after a few months.
20 to 30 min continuously skipping would be a nice goal for the first months, after that, increase when you feel like it. Right now I can skip 60 min continuously.
You should look up some easy exercises for when skipping (for example crossing your arms, switching legs...), that makes it less boring. You can also do for example 10 pullups every time you stumble over your cord, that's even better for your endurance and you get stronger.

it is VERY, no, EXTREMELY IMPORTANT to warm up trougly and stretch good before you skip for a long time, otherwise you ruin your legs. Warming up can be done by a little jogging, some burpees, some pushups, a little bit slow skipping...
For stretching: make sure you stretch al the muscles in your legs very well, your arms, back and stomach also wouldn't hurt. Turning with your wrists and ankles is very importannt as you mainly swing the rope with your wrists and jump by moving your ankles.

following up on the one limb effort ... one leg squats. I know I can't do one in the positive direction, but I can do them negatively. Eventually will get it positive.
Same with one armed pullups. Can't do it postitive, but can do eccentrics (negative).
I didn't want to defeat my own argument earlier, but now that I'm on the bandwagon ... I'll pull out the old argument that Isometric exercises can do wonders for nearly all muscle groups. It's one of the few ways you can exercise your hamstrings without equipment. (that is leg vs. leg with maximal effort for 6-10 seconds, employing varied angles).

With a chain firmly anchored somewhere, you can do a lot with a half hour of isometrics. It helps to know anatomy to understand what muscles you're trying to stress.
These, by the way, are the basis for Charles Atlas's dynamic tension ... which really do work, but only for the mentally tough who can truly push themselves, limb against limb.
