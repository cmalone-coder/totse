some of the more importiant things to remember when shooting long range are stability, and making sure that that stability does not come at the cost of something touching your barrel. never let your barrel touch anything when firing. a barrel works like a tuning fork when you fire a bullet through it. it vibrates at a certian harmonic pitch. if you touch the tines of a tuning fork while you tap it, it wont work. if you touch the barrel of a rifle when firing, it will throw off the harmonics and send the bullet off erratically. on modern sniping rifles, the forstock doesnt even touch the barrel. this keeps rounds from keyholin the papers as much.

What phrack is talking about is also called "free floating" your barrel. It is one of the easiest performed modifications you can do on many rifles. Basically it's this: remove the stock, sand out the material that surround the barrel. I stop when I can get an index card that's folded in half twice between the barrel and the stock. Some weapons don't even have a fore are or forestock.

I don't know what branch you are in phrack, however USMC snipers do not regularly carry a bipod, unless they are using the Barrett.

I suggest, Mack, a book on target shooting/sniping. Their is a difference, though each uses many of the same shooting techniques, sniping itself is a different field onto it's own. Snipers stay hidden not just by firing one shot, but also through very well thought out planning, movements, camoflauge, location, and timing. As a rule of thumb, a Marine Sniper told me, they seek out the nastiest place a person would not want to be in the area, that makes for an apparently difficult shot. Leave a week ahead of schedule and crawl to that spot at rate of a few inches per minute, when no one is looking.

I'm babbling now, but there is much more than sniping than just long range shooting.
If you don't plan on sniping but you feel you may have to engage in long range combat what you want to learn can be found in target manuals. Learn it well and learn to be stable, the movement you arm goes through because your heart beat, can throw you off a even a few inches at 1000m. Also obtaining and learning to use a quality scope with a range finding reticle and bullet drop compensation is nice, a range finding reticle is pretty much a necessity. Variable power is nice as well, but more moving parts=more chance for failure. I have 2 scopes now that are stuck because of rough handling. One is a Leupold! One is stuck on 6x, which ain't bad, the other on 12x, which really sucks.

Do you have a place you can shoot that far? Since I moved from Quantico, VA I am limited to 300m ranges, it's frustrating not being able to find a place to shoot out to 800m or so, safely.

i never was in the military persay, though i was the armorer for the western resistance militia for 2 years. and as far as silencers go, though i have made and used a few, i would never put one on an sws. the whole point behind a high power rifle is to be able to apply fire from a position thats far enough away that sound doesnt matter.

sounds travel in different ways and different directions. muzzle blast will bounce sound off at 180' from your position but the bullet sound will make em think your 90' from where you actually are. this happens because the sound of a bullet comes off the round from just behind the leading edge of the bullet, and travels 90' to the direction that the bullet is traveling.(not that any of this information is usefull in regulat target shooting)

if your interested in silencers, i would check out george hayduke's books on workshop supressors. he's got some good designs but few that would be suitable for a high powered rifles and none of them nesessary for sniping.

sniping takes alot of practice and even more experence. i forgot what the FM #s were but try to find the army S.O. sniper field training and employment manual. i have a copy of it around here somewhere but i dont feel like looking for it. the book has a distribution restriction notice so it might be hard to find. it also says on the cover, destroy by any means that will prevent disclosure of contents or reconstruction of the document.

Re: question
If it matters, the accuracy qualification for US army snipers is to put 8/10 shots with a .308 M40A1 into a 10 inch ring at 1000 yards within 30 minutes.

The stalking qualification is as follows: there is a bunker with two sniper trainers in it. The sniper trainee starts out 800 yards from the bunker, and has 6 hours to move to 150-100 yards from the bunker. If the sniper is spotted while moving, he has failed. Once he is within range, the sniper will fire one blank. If the sniper is spotted after he takes his shot, he has failed. If he still has not been spotted, he will take another shot. If the sniper is spotted after he takes his second shot, he has failed. If he still has not been spotted, another instructor will move to the sniper's position and place his hand on the sniper's head. If the instructors still cannot see the sniper, one of the instructors will write something (a word or a number) on a index card and hold it up for a moment. The sniper's rifle is then checked for windage and elevation settings to make sure that they are correct, and his sniper log is checked to see if he read the index card correctly. The sniper must succeed this test 2/3 times to graduate. The logic behind this is that if the sniper cannot be detected by the US's best snipers, he cannot be detected by anyone.

For practice, military snipers shoot at moving targets at 500, 600, and 800 yards, and stationary targets at 1000 and 1200 yards.

I also know of a SWAT team whose snipers shoot at 1.5 inch spinners at 600 yards with their .308 Robar SR-60s to practice.

thanks steelblade, i was gonna mention the weapon data cards if the conversation ever got there.

each sws (sniper weapon system) should also have a set of data cards that keep record of everything the rifle has ever done.

you have to record how many rounds have gone through that particular barrel and what kind of ammo of course but most importantly, you record performance.

you have to record things like where your cold bore, first shot landed as opposed to where you wanted it to land. you keep track of what temps. effect your rounds in what way. you have to record things like mirrage and how you compensated for it. and you do this for every round and every range.

every time you fire that weapon, you should have to write out a books worth of information.

the perpose for this is so that when your in the field and you have a stationary target at 600m, you can look in your data cards and say "ok, on the twelth of june, after almost the same amount of rounds through the barrel, at almost the same temp, with the same mirrage, and the wind going .5 nots faster but in the same direction, my cold bore first shot landed 2"down and 1"right from where i wanted it to go." it sounds like alot of figuring and it is.
