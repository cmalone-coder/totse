one explanation that fits the bill is not necessarily the correct explanation. as a chemist I would expect you to go by this, science is a body of knowledge built by questioning, theorizing, and testing.

For example, I've been doing research lately on electronically protecting steel from corroding in any way while immersed in a water emulsified bis-phenol epoxy resin, no co-solvents. A customer of ours has experienced problems of iron compound deposits on their parts. One thing we determined is that the [Fe] is high. The annealing which is black iron oxide, Fe2O4, is coming off of the steel. In a basic solution, we have proven that basic iron salts do form that most likely originate from interruption in the half reaction of red iron oxide, Fe2O3. Beyond that we have many theories that foot the bill perfectly, but when we apply methods that on paper should work we don't get nearly the expected results. We have several explanations that stoich out fine, but in real life don't make sense. I've talked to top metallurgists and polymer chemists in the area, all are PhD's with the Battelle Institute and Metss Corp. and can't seem to figure it out either. The only working solution is a quick fix that I feel will compound the problems later on down the road. I wish I could go into greater detail but most of the research is propietary as so are our resin systems.
It has lead to some good discoveries however in e-coating with resins and can improve sheen. I haven't yet determined though if this is caused by a reduction in surface tension or electrical attraction, I assume the latter, but will have to wait till my new toy, a tensiometer, comes in from Fisher.
