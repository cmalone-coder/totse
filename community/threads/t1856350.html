THE State Government has banned clubbing sick and injured Rottnest Island quokkas to death as a form of euthanasia and will fit rangers' vehicles with carbon dioxide gas units to do the job.

Tourism Minister Bob Kucera yesterday outlined a five-point plan for the humane and sustainable management of island quokkas.

The Government was forced to act after revelations in The West Australian that island rangers were clubbing injured and sick quokkas to death rather than sending them to a mainland sanctuary.

Mr Kucera said the new plan was developed in a meeting yesterday with animal and wildlife experts. It includes:



The use of carbon dioxide to euthanase dying quokkas. Mobile carbon dioxide units will be fitted to rangers' vehicles for use on isolated parts of the island.



Further training in wildlife care for Rottnest rangers.



Building a wildlife station on the island for sick and injured quokkas and other wildlife.



The Rottnest Island Authority to review guidelines for triage and management of sick and injured quokkas with animal welfare experts.



The authority to look at new education campaigns to get visitors to stop harming quokkas.

Mr Kucera told the Legislative Assembly yesterday experts agreed carbon dioxide was the most humane way to euthanase quokkas.

The Rottnest Island Foundation Conservation Trust would also play a stronger role in ensuring the future wellbeing of the animals.

Qualified veterinary nurse and CALM- contracted wildlife educator Liz Appelt said the package appeared to be a good start. But she was still concerned the quokkas would not be getting the best treatment.

Mrs Appelt, who voluntarily treated 30 injured, orphaned or sick quokkas which were sent to her at the Chidlow Marsupial Hospital from Rottnest, said it was essential for the animals to be cared for in the new wildlife station by full-time and highly-qualified animal welfare staff.

"Obviously people are starting to realise these animals do deserve better than they were getting," Mrs Appelt said.

RSPCA spokeswoman Kelly Oversby said the use of carbon dioxide was best in a tough situation.

Expert advice had shown her that previous preference for lethal injections was not the best option and the mobile units also offered less risk to operators.

She said the plan would benefit quokkas on the island and she hoped the RSPCA would be involved in tailoring further education processes.
