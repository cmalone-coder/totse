Ah, the old debate ... reminds me of the fact that I live with two feminist women and at least one still thinks she's entitled to educate me on everything in life.

If you were to define a feminist as a person who believes in equal rights and opportunities for women and men alike, then I'd have to say that yes, I am a feminist.

The problem, to be perfectly honest, is that in being a scientist and an engineer, I have to point out that there are some practical flaws with such an ideological concept.

For example -- there are what some people call bona fide qualifications for a given job or task. Firefighters, for example, go through physical training with certain requirements on upper-body strength and endurance. Somebody pointed out that the requirements as written were discriminatory against women, who for biological reasons do not have (at least on average) the same or the potential for the same upper-body strength. If I recall correctly, a woman sued over this in a human rights complaint and actually won -- in which case she was allowed to become a firefighter meeting lower physical standards, or the standards were lowered for either her or all women thereafter.

So it becomes, on occasion, a question of who is best suited for a particular occupation. We wouldn't call it discrimination if a man were to ask for equal rights to breastfeed an infant -- men simply aren't suited to the task. So why is it that when a given set of criteria happen to discriminate against women for reasons which are nobody's fault but nature's, that those criteria are called sexist?

For those who want to turn this into a debate on abortion rights, you can also browse through an article called A Man's Right to Choose by Thomas Lynch Milford. His arguments are interesting, to say the least, but illustrate how it is difficult to assign equal rights under the law to both men and women when men and women are so different.

Coupled with the idea of discrimination, of course, is the idea of reparation, which I absolutely disagree with. There is a clause, for example, in Canada's Charter of Rights and Freedoms, which allows certain instances of un-equal rights for the purpose of ameliorating the circumstances of a historically disadvantaged group, or something along those lines (That is, for all intents and purposes, an Affirmative Action clause).

It's one thing to say that men, women, blacks, whites, Asians, Pygmies, Australian-aborigines, straights, gays, bisexuals, transgendered people, Christians, Jews, Muslims, Buddhists, Shintoists, Baha'is, Hindus, and everybody else are equal in principle and deserve equal opportunities both to better themselves and to prove themselves worthy at home, at work, or at play -- but it's quite another thing to say that because women or any other 'historically disadvantaged group' have had a rough time at the hands of the majority for thousands upon thousands of years, they should have the playing field tipped in their favor today.

Granted, everybody should have equal access to the resources available to them. If a white person can get a student loan more easily than a black person with the same grades, then that's wrong.

But to say that I -- a person who has never demeaned or oppressed a woman in his life, who has never owned any human property or enslaved any person of color not his own, who has never denied a person outside of the religious majority in his country the same rights as he, and who has never looked down on non-heterosexuals as sub-human or perverse -- am somehow responsible for the plight of said individuals and must therefore make some kind of restitution by a) having my job application given lesser consideration, b) being passed up for promotion, or c) being overlooked for a scholarship, simply because, by random chance, I happen to be white and male, flies in the face of the idea of equal rights.
