Shrooms of a different nature
No, not the ones you eat. The ones you look at after you eat the other ones...

Since this forum is presumably home to the great majority of Totse's mycologists, I present you with this:

ALTERNATIVE SHROOMAGE!

Imagine if you will: A friend calls, and invites you over. He sounds a bit excited, and you stash the bong and stumble over to his apartment. He meets you at the door and you go inside. A glass/lexan box, perhaps 50cm high and wide, twice that measure in length, sits on the coffee table. Inside is a partially decayed log, resting in wood chips. Covering this material are dozens of clusters of mushrooms, their offwhite, fan-shaped caps jutting randomly from the log. This in itself is a somewhat unusual centerpiece. Your host mentions offhand that they are Panellus stipticus, and you quickly check your mental notebook and fail to recognize any psychedelic significance. "That last blotter sheet was too much," you think, "he's lost it..."

He reaches over and turns off the lights.

"HOLY SHIT! THEY FUCKING GLOW IN THE DARK!"

Before you, the case is illuminated with a soft green-blue glow as the fruited shrooms luminesce.

"Technically," he grins, "they glow all the time. You just couldn't see it before..."

Yes, boys and girls, nature was nice enough to provide three treats within the fungi kingdom. You're surely familiar with pizza-class shrooms, and those of the "visionary" persuasion, but what about the foxfire of lore? Another shroom, Armillaria mellea (notable for being the largest organism on the planet- colonies of clones can span several acres), is bioluminescent, but it is only the mycelium that glows- the fruiting bodies are just boring old shrooms.

So, my fellow fungus fanciers, I give you a mission! Go forth and fructify! The Sporeworks will happily deliver the necessary supplies to your door, even if you live in the spore-smuggling deserts of California or Georgia. Share your fungal feats with us!

Re: Shrooms of a different nature
My mind's started turning over. I'll never get to sleep at this rate...

Ok. First off, how to begin the culture? Logic suggests that we start the process like any other, inoculating individual jars. This keeps the variables to a minimum, and ensure that any contaminated specimens are easily segregated. Successful growths can then be transferred to the "box". An overturned aquarium would seem ideal for this. A bit heavy, but pleasant to look at. A custom lexan/acrylic box would be ideal.

Prepping the box would certainly require some work. A log would certainly look better that simple wood shavings/chips. One would need to find a way to remove as many insects as possible, as I would guess that they would attract undesirable bacteria. The entire box could be filled, sealed, and then steam sterilized like a low-pressure autoclave. I don't know how much pressure aquariums are designed to take, but 15psi/ (a number I've seen tossed around for the pressure-cooker sterilization techniques) is attained at something like 35 feet of water, and while the aquarium can certainly cope with 2 feet, I have to wonder...

The problem, though, is that I'm really only familiar with Tek-style growing. That's designed to produce big yields, not pretty ones. I don't know how many flushes a log could sustain.

In any case, the cakes would then be strategically placed (or crumbled, perhaps?) in the aquarium. Here's where the risk comes in. In order to make things look nice, you want large patches of shrooms. One would leave the cakes in the aquarium, and keep it dark and warm to promote the growth of the mycelium into the additional substrate. When things are sufficiently covered, we expose the colony to the world and let the flush begin.

Several problems and/or questions remain, though.

<UL TYPE=SQUARE>

<LI>Will the mycelium easily graft itself onto the wood?

<LI>What if evil mold attacks the colony? You can't just remove a cake when everything's attached to a hunk of wood.

<LI>When does one remove the fruiting bodies? Unlike psilocybes, we want them to hang around as long as possible. What happens when the fruit dies? Does this promote the intrusion of "bad things"?

<LI>How does the mycelium handle constant fruiting? Does it simply exhaust the food supply, or is there some sort of damage done by keeping our fungus constantly pregnant?

</UL>
