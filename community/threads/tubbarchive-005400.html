Rules are the same as last week (even if I did not always abide by the explaining rule). This weeks topic is;
Rule of Law: Basis of authority.

The question I am asking is, what is the best form of governmental law... or in different terms how should a government excersize it's authority over it's people. Should there be a written constitution, enshrining the fundementals of law binding all subsequent generation? Should law be determined without any guidlines, by a congress or executive leader? The options are limitless, so let's hear you opinions.

Personally I am a firm believer in Constitutionalism. I think there needs to be a firm law, outlining the basic rules of government and rights of the people. Of course statutory law should be developed within the congress and be due to change. Without a "supreme law" or document the government is going to be prone to consistent abuses of it's citizens and the government it's self would be unsustainable. To easily would one person or group of people be able to rise to the top and gain control that they would not readily cede.

Because we cannot trust a government to treat it's subjects with deserved dignity, nor can we trust individuals to not usurp ultimate authority from the government, thereby creating a dictatorship. Therefore the rules and guidlines limiting and granting governmental power must, ultimately, be enshrined within a constitution.

As outlined by the constitution itself the American constitution is actually quite difficult to change (in fact, it's one of the hardest in the world to modify). There are different ways for this to be done and two seperate levels which it must go through. Initially it has to be proposed to the states for ratification. The most used way of doing this is for an amendment to obtain a super-majority (66 percent) of both houses (the House of Representatives and the US Senate). The second way to do this has only been used once and that was when the constitution was originally presented, this is for a Constitutional convention to be called. The states each send representatives to the convention and the convention is then used to draft and propose amendments to the states (again, I believe, requiring the "super majority").

The second step in the process is for the states to ratify or reject the proposed amendments... again there are two ways of doing this. The first way is for 75 percent of the state legislature to accept the proposed amendment, in which case the amendment becomes part of the constitution (succesfully used 16 times). The second way is for the states to call special ratifying conventions, if 75 percent of the ratifying conventions the amendment is then, again, succesfully adopted (used once, the 21st amendment).

As you can see it's actually quite difficult to change the constitution (as it should be) because the constitution lays down the fundemental laws of the nation. To date only 27 amendments have been succesfully ratified, not counting the original ten amendments that makes seventeen. Over a span of roughly 220 years I would say thats pretty stable.

Now each individual state has it's own state constitution and usually these are much less stable. Take Texas for example, it's constitution is 80000 or so words long and contains over 400 amendments... this is the seventh constituion. The Federal constitution (for a reference point) is a mere 3000 words long and has been in effect since the collapse of the articles of confederation.

Using the US example one could say that, by far, constitutional governments are superior to other forms. However, there is a caution here. The US constitution is actually a rare example as most constitutions are changed every few decades, summarily ignored and overly complex (often including statutory laws). France for example has had five seperate constitutions and the Brazilian constitutions call for everyone to have a house has been ruled as an "Ideal" and not law thereby making an arguement that their entire constitution is an "Ideal" and not law. While constitutions are important the must also be difficult to change and contain only the fundemental principles of the government. Otherwise they end up being nothing more than a useless piece of paper.
