I have done lots of research on this subject. First of all, it is in no way a closed loop system. You will need to swap batteries from time to time, and you possibly need to use a dedicated battery to run the entire electrolysis apparatus, to make things last longer.

The method will work, but it wont work for too lng since your combustion chamber will rust very quickly under power from this stuff. You will need the ceramic coating the paper talks about.

The electrolysis chamber wont be that much of a problem to make extremely efficient. I dont know why you would think so. If you have 12 volts accross eight plate in series, 1.5 volts is dropped accross the individual plate pairs. This is enough to be considered very efficient and still leave a margin for voltage discharge from the battery.

Your elecrolysis chamber is going to be the most important feature of your setup. One of the main limiting factors for efficiency is gas buildup on the plates. If you have ever conducted an electrolysis experiment with plates other than platinum or porous metal, you notice the gas bubbles like to build up on the plates. Gas bubbles are non conductive, so your resistance builds up very quickly. This transforms into a very lossy system since the reaction is more efficient with greater current. More resistance, less current, less efficiency.

I think I have solved this though without resorting to extremely expensive platinum or porous steel. Think for a second. Where have you seen somethin that keeps the water swirling with no mechanical device to move the water by force? Ultrasonic piezo ring a bell?

I propose that when I build one (I am really hoping this summer) I want to get some piezos so they will shake the bubbles off the plates. Problem solved.

Next is pulse wave modulation. For my setup, I will use a microcontroller to monitor the entire system for pressure, malfunctions, and monitor input from whatever I use to modulate the throttle. I dont like the idea of the PWM being tied to the throttle, however. I would like to keep the PWM isolated from the throttle at all times. How I want to set it up as, is that the throttle is unmonitored, but the microcontroller senses a drop in fuel pressure and then increases the PWM duty cycle until the pressure is restored. This will take lots of trial and error to get perfect though, since not only do I have to modulate how the controller drives the electrolyzer, but I also must adjust the fuel/air trim on the carb. Those two factors are dependant on each other so finding the right combination may take some time.

Im out of comments for the moment, if you have anymore questions, feel free to post them.

You raise an interesting question. I think that it deserves some looking into, but at first glance, you can say that most likely the reaction is more efficient when it is cold. The water is slightly (the coefficient of expansion is small) more dense, and the resistance is much lower, therefore you get more gas because of the higher current and higher density.'

When you combust something, you want it to be as cold as possible. I am thinking water injection, but I would have to get a heavy engine for that. Anyway, for best Carnot efficiency, you want the temp to be as low as possible:

100% * (T_cold - T_hot)/T_hot

This equatin gives you the greatest efficiency of any heat exchange engine.

The water cannot be broken under normal voltages if it is in a vapor. It doesnt have any conducting electrolytes, so it makes a damn nice dielectric.

I would think that the reacton at high pressure, would force more gas into the water than is necessary. That displaces electrolytes and makes the water less dense. Thus it probably increases resistance by some marginal amount.




Something I want to point out. Potassium hydroxide is the cheapest best electrolyte available. There are a couple that are better, but this is cheap and is in the top ten best. At %25 molarity, it reaches its greatest conductivity. It also is very stable accross time, but will need to be replaced ever so often.

Another thing to keep in mind: after a while, electrolytes and plates are likely to plate themselves to your plates, so you will need to change polarity at regular intervals. After some time of driving, they need to be switched. That wont be hard at all, consdering I can multiplex inputs to the drivers very easilly through the controller.

quote:
Conventional electrolysis cells are capable of producing hydrogen and oxygen from water. These conventional cells generally include two electrodes arranged within the cell which apply energy to the water to thereby produce hydrogen and oxygen. The two electrodes are conventionally made of two different materials.

However, the hydrogen and oxygen generated in the conventional cells are generally produced in an inefficient manner. That is, a large amount of electrical power is required to be applied to the electrodes in order to produce the hydrogen and oxygen. Moreover, a chemical catalyst such as sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide must be added to the water to separate hydrogen or oxygen bubbles from the electrodes. Also, the produced gas must often be transported to a pressurized container for storage, because conventional cells produce the gases slowly. Also, conventional cells tend to heat up, creating a variety of problems, including boiling of the water. Also, conventional cells tend to form gas bubbles on the electrodes which act as electrical insulators and reduce the function of the cell.

Accordingly, it is extremely desirable to produce a large amount of hydrogen and oxygen with only a modest amount of input power. Furthermore, it is desirable to produce the hydrogen and oxygen with "regular" tap water and without any additional chemical catalyst, and to operate the cell without the need for an additional pump to pressurize it. It would also be desirable to construct the electrodes using the same material. Also, it is desirable to produce the gases quickly, and without heat, and without bubbles on the electrodes.

Orthohydrogen and parahydrogen are two different isomers of hydrogen. Orthohydrogen is that state of hydrogen molecules in which the spins of the two nuclei are parallel. Parahydrogen is that state of hydrogen molecules in which the spins of the two nuclei are antiparallel. The different characteristics of orthohydrogen and parahydrogen lead to different physical properties. For example, orthohydrogen is highly combustible whereas parahydrogen is a slower burning form of hydrogen. Thus, orthohydrogen and parahydrogen can be used for different applications. Conventional electrolytic cells make only orthohydrogen and parahydrogen. Parahydrogen, conventionally, is difficult and expensive to make.

Accordingly, it is desirable to produce cheaply orthohydrogen and/or parahydrogen using a cell and to be able to control the amount of either produced by the cell. It is also desirable to direct the produced orthohydrogen or parahydrogen to a coupled machine in order to provide a source of energy for the same.
