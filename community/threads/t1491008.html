Question one: How do you define rape?

Rape, is the penetration of one of the victim's bodily orifice with the hands, fingers, penis, etc.

OR

the use of one of the victim's body parts in the penetration of one of the perpetrator's bodily orifices,

THEREIN

with the intent to sexually humiliate the victim or to otherwise denegrate or humiliate the victim for the sexual gratification of the perpetrator

OR

where both parties have not given informed, rational, affirmative consent to the acts in question.

Although rape as most commonly committed is unquestionably a crime of violence, it is distinguished from battery or grievous bodily harm in that the sexual aspect touches on the innermost parts of the victim's psychological health and their need to have their sexuality held inviolate.

Question two: How do you define physical abuse?

Abuse implies a long-term infliction of wrong against another person, versus the term 'molestation', for example. Physical abuse, therefore, is the actual physical touching of another person with the intent of controlling their behavior or reshaping their attitudes, often with the intent to harm the person in the process. Sexual abuse can be, but is not necessarily, physical abuse as well.

Question three: How do you define sexual harrassment?

Harrassment implies the use of physical or verbal media to make another person feel vulnerable, demeaned, disgusted, unworthy, threatened, or otherwise uncomfortable. Often it is the only crime or tort by which a person can make another feel bad without resorting to battery or assault. Sexual harrassment uses sexual overtones or sexual aspects in its delivery. It can be, but may not be limited to, crude sexual jokes & conversation, sexual touching, and propositioning.

Question four: How many people do you know who have been raped? About what age were they when it happened?

I heard of one case which might have been rape (since the victim didn't relay the details of the attack) but she would have been perhaps as young as six, whereas her attacker was 8, and someone she knew.

Question five: Whose responsibility do you think it is to keep rape from happening?

Arguably, any crime is the sole responsibility of the perpetrator, but we have to consider that rape is a crime perpetrated largely by (male) individuals against other (female) individuals, usually when both individuals know each other. That and the fact that rapists don't believe they have any responsibility at all to not commit that particular crime.

So the rapist doesn't exist in a vacuum -- crime requires both means, motive, and opportunity. Motive can't be controlled, as it's firmly in the domain of the perpetrator. Keeping rape from happening requires that we (and by that I mean anybody who is a member of civilized society and is capable under the circumstances) have to recognize when the means & opportunity exist, and to remove them. By that I mean to keep people from obtaining rape drugs, deadly weapons, etc. and also from keeping potential victims from harm's way by having things like more cops on the street, campus patrols, always walking in groups, etc.

Question six: What makes rape different from sex?

Sex requires two people capable of giving informed, rational, mutual consent to the sexual act. Any sexual act without mutual or rational or informed consent is, by definition, rape.

Question seven: Why do you think rape is such a hot topic on college campuses?

Probably because college is the first time where young adults feel a genuine sense of self-empowerment to experience the various aspects of life.

Arguably, it's at this point where things can go wrong the most, when people can be lulled into a false sense of security when overcome by their curiosity (i.e. first-timers). Naive victims / clever perpetrators are just as likely to meet in this kind of environment as well-intentioned partners who may be unaware of the bounds of proper conduct. quote:(...)the idea that a guy who lets a girl have her way with him is somehow morally deprived or lacking, especially when they're both at least buzzing, is hard to swallow.

A term often used in civil law, especially where responsibility is concerned, is "duty of care." I have, for example, a duty of care to ensure that the things I design and construct aren't prone to electrocuting people. The average citizen has a duty of care to ensure that his/her pets aren't allowed off-leash in public, to ensure that chemicals and medicines are kept out of reach of young children when appropriate, and so forth. In the event that a person disregards their duties of care, they can be held responsible for whatever happens as a result.

When we're talking about criminal law, the 'duty of care' principle applies more often to inaction rather than action (and this is because crime is normally the outright commission of a wrongdoing rather than the classic definition of a tort, which is the failure to commit a right-doing where appropriate) and yes, there are many instances where inaction on the part of a reasonably-minded person is criminal.

As hard as it might be to swallow, the law doesn't take a bright view of a bystander allowing a drowing person to perish, and nor does it apparently take a bright view of a man (with enough of his faculties intact) sitting absolutely still and silent while some woman strips naked and gives him a thorough screwing after downing seven martinis.
