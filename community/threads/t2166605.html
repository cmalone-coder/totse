The Life of Frederick Douglass: Essay

How does Douglasss narrative make use of examples to develop persuasive arguments against slavery in chapters 1-4?

Douglasss narrative makes good use of examples to develop a strong argument against slavery by showing his readers (a skeptical audience) what slavery was all about. At that time, most people had the wrong idea about slavery. They did not know specific details about slavery; they cant get any closer than Slaves work on a plantation for their master. Their master feeds them and gives them shelter. Slaves are tools. They are made for working. No one knew what really happens on plantations and other places where slaves work. The use of specific details and evidence in the narrative clears up most misconceptions that people have about slavery, and works as an effective argument against slavery.

The introductory paragraph sums up the main idea of this narrative quite nicely. Slave owners intentionally withheld information from their slaves, to keep them ignorant. The slaves knew no more about their history or age than an animal would. The owners believed that the slaves would be happy and content if they were kept in mental darkness, but most slaves were unhappy due to this lack of information. Frederick Douglass is unable to tell his exact age, because he does not know it. Virtually all slaves dont know their ages.

Chapter 1:

In the first chapter, Douglass shows the reader that slaves are not born slaves. They are made into slaves. The separation of a child from its mother shortly after birth disrupts the development of affection between child and parent. Inevitably, the child has no special feelings toward its mother, and the mother eventually loses interest in the child (the mother stops brooding over the separation of the child). Frederick Douglass uses his own past as an example of this. However, Fredericks mother still had strong affection for her son. Frederick did not. His mother traveled 12 miles during the night (as told by Douglass) to see her child. They never communicated. His mother simply tucked him in to bed, and ran the 12 miles back to her master to avoid being flogged in the morning. When his mother died, Douglass received the tidings of her death with much the same emotions he would have felt at the death of a stranger. That shows the toll of enslavement on Douglasss soul.

Douglass was born in Tuckahoe, Talbot County, Maryland.

Also, Douglass says that he did not know who his father was, but he was probably his first master, Captain Anthony. He says that slave owners who impregnated female slaves did it for two reasons: to gratify their horrid sexual desires (that their white wives cannot satiate), and to increase the amount of slaves they have. Mulatto slaves were usually treated more cruelly than black slaves. The white mistress (whose husband impregnated the mother of the mulatto child) was envious of the slave mother. She was happy only when the mulattoes suffered. Ironically, the selling of mulatto children to other slave owners is an act of humanity. That means that the mulattoes will not be bothered by their father, the mistress, or their white siblings.

The large amount of mulatto children in the South goes against the foundation of slavery! The Bible says that Ham and his descendents are cursed (so that all Africans can be enslaved, for they are not human). If slaves are not human, then why are white men impregnating female slaves? It just shows that slave owners dont care if slaves are human or not human; they just want a group of people (that everyone dislikes) to be their slaves. They just want workers, and as many of them as possible. Greed (and barbarity) is the foundation of slavery. Pretending that slaves are not human is just a ploy to make most people hate slaves, while those that profit from the slaves know the truth.

The final part of Chapter 1 involves Douglasss first taste of brutality on the plantation, where his Aunt Hester is gratuitously beaten by her master, for disobeying his orders. This incident is so significant to Douglass because he had never seen anything like it before, for he has always stayed out of trouble (and never witnessed the beatings) on the plantation. He says that this act of savagery has opened the gate to the hell that is slavery, and prepared him for many more beatings and acts of savagery that would occur in the future. After that ordeal, Douglass hid himself in a closet, and would not come out for a long time after the beating ended.

As you can see, the large amount of specific examples used in this chapter (and narrative) makes for an effective argument against slavery.

Chapter 2:

This chapter is also full of specific examples and imagery. It begins with a description of Colonel Lloyd, Master Anthony, his family, and his plantations. Colonel Lloyd was a rich landowner in Talbot County, Maryland (the county in which Douglass was born). He owned hundreds of slaves, and many farms.

Douglasss first master was Lloyds clerk and superintendent. He was the overseer of the overseers. He lived with his family in a house on Colonel Lloyds home plantation (the Great House Farm). All major transactions go through this plantation, including the slaves rations. Any disputes or arguments (including rowdy slaves) were settled at Lloyds home plantation.

The overseer of the home plantation was called Mr. Severe. Douglass says that this was a good name for him, for he was a cruel man. He had no morals, and took great pleasure in beating slaves. He died shortly after Douglass came to Lloyds home plantation. The slaves believed that his death was some sort of merciful providence. He was replaced by Mr. Hopkins, a calm and humane man. He was considered as a good overseer.

Slaves received their allowances of food and clothes at the Great House Farm. They received food once a month, and clothes once a year. If these clothes wore out or were somehow destroyed, they would be naked or partially clothed until the next allowance-day. As told by Douglass, it was a common sight to see partially-naked slaves on the plantation. Children that could not work did not receive an allowance of clothes. They received 2 coarse shirts per year.

Slaves that lived on the farms owned by Colonel Lloyd believed that being chosen to work on the Great House Farm was a great privilege. Slaves chosen to go there sing songs to express their joy in going to the Great House Farm. As a slave, Douglass said that he did not understand the meaning of these slave songs. He is astonished that some people in the North believe that these slave songs show the happiness of slaves, when these songs really show the deepest anguish in the slaves. Chapter 3:

This chapter begins with a long description of Colonel Lloyds garden, which was famed for having delicious fruits. Many slaves (especially children) tried to steal fruits from the garden to supplement their insufficient diets. They were caught and whipped. Since the slaves became such a threat to his garden, the colonel took many measures to end the theft. He finally succeeded when he had his workers put tar all over the garden fence. Any slave that had tar on himself was severely whipped. Soon, the slaves began to fear the tar as much as they feared the whip.

Colonel Lloyd had horses that were of the finest breed, and a riding carriage. He assigned two slaves (old Barney and young Barney) to take care of the horses. If anything was wrong with the horses, whether they didnt move fast enough, or they looked as though they were not properly cared for, he blamed the slaves. Nothing could shield them from any accusation made by the colonel. The slightest inattention to the horses meant that the slaves would be severely punished. This is ironic because his slaves are treated like disposable tools, and his horses are treated like divine beings (meaning that everything must be perfect for the horses).

Colonel Lloyds family always enjoyed the luxury of whipping his slaves. Douglass himself has witnessed many beatings of the slaves. Its almost as if punishing slaves was an enjoyable family activity.

Many people estimated that Lloyd owned more than 1,000 slaves. Douglass believes that this is an accurate estimate. In this chapter, Douglass wrote of an incident that proved that Colonel Lloyd owned so many slaves that he did not know them all personally, and vice versa.

Once, a slave walking down a road was addressed by Colonel Lloyd. After asking him several questions (Who is your master? Does he treat you well?, etc.), he found out that the slave had found fault with him. Instead of telling the slave that he is his master, he simply rode on (on his horse). The slave thought nothing more of the incident, until a few weeks later. He was informed that, for having found fault with his master, he was now to be sold to a Georgia trader. After this incident, many slaves began lying about their true feelings, to avoid punishment, for slave masters sometimes have spies to record what their fellow slaves are saying. This is another piece of evidence to suggest that slaves are human. No animal could lie to avoid the consequences! Douglass himself admits that, as a slave, he has never spoken negatively about his masters.

Slaves were always quarreling about who has the better master. Colonel Lloyds slaves always boasted that Colonel Lloyd was a richer man. Jacob Jepsons (another slave owner) slaves boasted that Jepson was a better man. The slaves believed that the characteristics of their masters somehow applied to themselves. As said by Douglass, It was considered as being bad enough to be a slave; but to be a poor mans slave was deemed a disgrace indeed!

Chapter 4:

Chapter 4 is significant because it shows the reader that the worst aspect of slavery is that it is not controlled by any law, or any social control. Slavery is a completely different domain. Anything can happen in slavery without consequences from outside sources. Douglass did a good job in this chapter. The use of specific examples shows that slavery is really evil (as said above), and makes his narrative more credible.

Apparently, Mr. Hopkins (overseer) was not severe enough to suit Colonel Lloyd. He was soon replaced by Mr. Austin Gore, a man who possessed all those traits of character indispensable to what is called a first-rate overseer. He was the most dreaded overseer (by the slaves). Douglass describes Mr. Gore in great detail (to add credibility to his narrative, and to prepare the reader for an atrocity discussed later in the chapter). Douglass implies that slavery has created Mr. Gore, a monster. Mr. Gore seemed to be less human than the slaves on the plantation. He was a brutal machine; a machine that inflicts terror into slaves. His sole purpose in life was to be a perfect overseer. He may have succeeded.

Later in the chapter, Mr. Gore whips a slave named Demby, who runs away and jumps into a creek to cauterize the skin. Gore gave him 3 calls to come out, before he shot him with a musket. Even Colonel Lloyd was shocked by this atrocity, and asked Gore why he did it. He replied that Demby became unmanageable and was a dangerous example to the other slaves. If Demby was unpunished, the other slaves would follow his example, and there would be no more order (as if there was any) on the plantation. Lloyd finds this defense satisfactory. What makes this incident even more appalling is that Gore is rewarded for killing Demby. His fame as an excellent overseer spread.

If Douglass had ended the chapter here, with no further evidence of barbarity, the readers of this narrative may have believed that Mr. Gore was just an insane man, and Demby was unfortunate enough to have crossed him. He shows that incidents like this occurred constantly. Slaves were constantly killed in gruesome ways, and no one did anything about it. Douglass continues the chapter with more specific examples.

About the same time as the murder of Demby, another slave was shot to death after unknowingly setting foot on another slave owners property. The slave owner came to see Colonel Lloyd about the murder, and the matter was soon hushed up. It was a common saying, among white people, that it was worth a half-cent to kill a nigger, and another half-cent to bury one.

Another slave owner, Mr. Thomas Lanman, killed two slaves. One of them died a particularly gruesome death- he was killed with an axe. Lanman is never prosecuted for these acts of inhumanity. He even brags about this deed, saying that everyone should do what he has done!

Another awful murder resulted in a warrant for a womans arrest (but was never issued). Mrs. Hicks killed a young female slave with an oak stick after she failed to respond to the babys crying. These incidents led Douglass to conclude that killing a slave, or any colored person, in Talbot County, Maryland, is not treated as a crime, either by the courts, or the community.

This chapter could possibly be the most effective argument against slavery. It shows the true evil in slavery.
