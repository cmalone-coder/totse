I chose Ubuntu.

It is a fork, and with forks come alterations and modifications not found in mainstream distributions, meaning Gentoo, Fedora/RHEL, Debian and Slackware.

Forks teach uncommon practices proprietary to that particular distribution, while not everything may be different, it is easier to learn a fork, than to unlearn a fork, especially any of them which give the appearance to be "easier" for "newbs".

I started using Linux with Slackware.
I like Fedora/RHELs design goals and they are constantly pushing out new technology faster than any other distribution, while Gentoo may also be on that same path.

These aspects give way to bug reporting, and feature requests which eventually make it into other distributions which use older software.

I think any distribution which ships with anything prorietary or copyright, has the potential to become a legal risk when distributing.
Fedora is free from that, and can be shipped anywhere in the world.
If the previous poster's comments are correct in that "Everything woks out of the box with Ubuntu", he is probably referring to external packages which may need installation on another system, which causes Ubuntu to become a potential, illegal item in some countries, depending on teh exact nature of the software provided and the patents, laws or other aspects regarding infringements and things.

In my experience, starting off using mainstream distributions is the best way to learn a system, than to start with a fork. While learning may not be someone's goal to use Linux, it cannot be avoided really.

No matter what Linux distribution you use, you will be required to learn something about it, even if it is Ubuntu.

Ubuntu is a Debian fork. Forks can be good, and Debian is a good distribution.
