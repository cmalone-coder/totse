Re: How much can you compromise in a relationship?
She should accept your drug use to a point where it begins to interfere in a negative way in her life... and that also means your life because when you're with someone your life tends to have a pretty big impact on theirs. She has the choice of whether or not to remain with you even though you use, but she doesn't have a choice of whether or not you use.

If you give it up without being asked, just because you know it bothers someone you love, then good for you. But the decision is completely yours.

If you actually do manage to keep things under control (especially your temperment, are you on an even emotional keel when you're using?) then I don't see any reason for her to complain, but keep in mind that your perception may be skewed. She may be able to see an oncoming problem before you do, this doesn't mean she's out to ruin your fun, she just cares.

I used to be uncomfortable with the fact that my partner liked to drink. Mine was an opinion formed of inexperience and naivety. Once I tried it out for myself, and spent time with him while he was out drinking a few times I recognized that he was in control, and that I didn't need to worry about it. Perhaps you need to include her more, have her around when you're all fucked up. If things really are 'ok' as you say they are then she'll recognize that and will be able to relax some.

Speaking from experience, there's a lot of inherent problems in a relationship where two people, on some fundamental level, don't see eye-to-eye. For example, my second long-term relationship (about 2 years long) was with a pretty, intelligent, and sensitive woman.

Major problems arose from the fact that a) I'm pretty much agnostic, and b) she's hovering somewhere amongst the various evangelical Protestant denominations (originally, she was Pentecostal).

There were days when we would talk about our futures as far ahead as getting married and having kids, but the sticking point would always be that I believe religion should be a personal choice, whereas she believed that any kids of hers would be raised Pentecostal from day zero. There was also some stuff about a lifelong attempt to save my soul ('cause, obviously, if I wasn't a believer and didn't care that I wasn't, it was Hell for sure).

For your own part, just thank the powers that be for the fact that it's not something that manifests itself in absolutes. If she can't stand much more than weed, but can stand weed, then that's a plus. She could be of the opinion that to save you from yourself, she should turn you in to the feds.

Part of the problem here, I think, is that you need to look at the situation more empathetically. Sure, you think that drugs are innocent and harmless ... and granted, they can be both in sufficiently capable hands ... but obviously her point of view is different.

Some, at this point, might be tempted to think that the solution is obvious: Get her to see things from your point of view. That, however, is something which can be strived for but never achieved. People generally can only come to a personal change of view if they want to.

So what do we do? We each accept that the other person has a right to their own point of view but, for the sake of cooperation and the future of the relationship at large, we need to modify our behavior.

For you to say, "Look baby, I still think that drugs are harmless and innocent enough, but in respect of the fact that you think they can be harmful, for you I'm going to cut back to only smoking weed," would illustrate a) your assertion of your right to an independent stance, b) your willingness to compromise in such a fashion that you both get part of what you want, and c) your desire to value your mutual relationship over your desire to use all the drugs you want.

That, of course, is just a suggestion.

Re: How much can you compromise in a relationship?
The drug thing is, or at least can be, a "caring about habits" issue. It mainly depends on the extent of the problems it's causing, and why the non-using partner doesn't like it. If she thinks, "Drugs are bad! No boyfriend of mine is going to do that shit," and constantly threatens to dump him over it, that's a control thing. If she has a legitimate concern, then it's probably more of an issue with what could happen to him.

The tough thing about defining any of this is that it's all a mstter of degrees. Few people launch into an anti-drug stance solely based on their need for control, although what Cindy often says about being conditioned to think drugs are bad is true a lot of the time. Still, the vast majority think they're being helpful and caring in trying to get a partner to stop using, so the most they can often be faulted for is cluelessness.
