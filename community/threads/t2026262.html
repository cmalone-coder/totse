Political Idols: Who?
Who are your greatest political influences? Mine are:

(I may add to this list, depending on the popularity of the thread, as I have many more influences than this.)

Thomas Paine: Tom Paine argues fantastically well against the anachronistic idiocy of monarchy, and is a powerful advocate for the inalienable rights of individuals, why democracy trumps its opposition, and why reason and rationality in public life is the noblest objective. I think Paine is still relevant today, especially when you consider that my own country hasn’t yet ditched the British monarch. Tools.

Karl Marx: Though I’m not a commie myself, or, indeed, even a socialist, you’d be an idiot to deny Marx’s influence, or his incredible intellect. And despite his predictions never actually coming to fruition, the man had incredible foresight in terms of how he saw the capitalist world emerging: “All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned”. I think that sentence sums up the globalization and the marketisation of the world brilliantly. I also agree with the idea of historical materialism, the idea that our social and economic status defines us.

Friedrich Hayek: The antithesis of Marx. The anti-socialist’s anti-socialist. I am not a classical liberal, and thus not a Hayekian, but am generally very sympathetic to Hayek and his ideas. I think The Road to Serfdom was a brilliant book, The Constitution of Liberty, also good, but very dense. I think Hayek suffered from the academic’s tendency to get too theoretical, and thereby shun reality in such a way as to make your ideas intangible. But still brilliant.

Irving Kristol: Kristol is a great essayist on topics such as capitalism, religion, American conservatism, and morality. I don’t much like his or the neo-conservatives’ views on foreign policy, because they are generally utopian and not grounded in reality, as Iraq testifies to, but Kristol is still brilliant, especially when arguing for capitalism. In fact, I’ve not read a more persuasive advocate for capitalism; he argues for it in moral terms, not in terms of efficiently or rationality, as is the wont of libertarians and the like.

Paul Keating: Americans and Europeans would likely be unfamiliar with this man, but he was treasurer of Australia from 1983 until 1991, and prime minister from 1991 until 1996. The man, quite frankly, was brilliant. He was able to articulate the need for economic reform to the imperative of social necessity. This may seem simple, but it’s not. He was also treasurer and prime minister of the country whilst representing the Labor Party, ostensibly a left-of-center party, and introduced a neo-liberal agenda, hardly an easy task for a left-wing government. Keating was actually one of Tony Blair’s intellectual idols, one of the people on whom Blair modeled ‘New Labour’.

Margaret Thatcher: I think Thatcher is the man because she was fucking tough, and also really, really tough. In an era such as ours in which politicians are basically stage-managed clods who fear stepping out of line, it was refreshing to have somebody like Thatcher, who really ran her own race. She implemented a Hayekian agenda, trashed the unions, made Britain more of an individualistic country, and showed in an age of stupid feminism that meritocracy always trumps: Thatcher proved that people succeed on their merits, not on their gender.
