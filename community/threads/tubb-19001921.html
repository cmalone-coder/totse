Fun with a Tire Valve
����I built me a new toy. It might even be something that would interest you.



����It's an extremely simple and rather small but very effective air pressure cannon, built from PVC pipe and a few other scraps. To those of you that engage in building these sorts of cannons mine is undoubtedly small potatoes (excuse the pun), but what's notable about this one is the fact that it cost me nothing at all to build. That, and I got it completely airtight. First try.
����I had the pipe and the tire valve kicking around already, as well as one of the reducer pieces. The rest of the materials the local Home Depot was kind enough to... donate. Either way, you're looking at paying about fifteen dollars to build a cannon like this, including the cost of full lengths of both diameters of pipe.
����To pressurize the cannon air is pumped in through a heavy duty schrader valve (originally intended to find a home on the wheel rim of a truck) with a bike pump:

����The valve can take 150 PSI safely. The same rating applies for the ball valve, which is the heart of the operation:

����Ball valves have to be actuated mechanically and are the lowest-tech way to operate your cannon. They're cheap and plentiful, though, as well as tough as nails and rated for some truly insane pressures. This valve is a half inch model - I was originally going to use a three-quarters but all the valves I could find were too hard to open quickly.
����All of this is bound to a detachable half inch barrel (I have a three-quarters barrel, as well) with no less than five reducing pieces. The chamber pipe is two inches, and the valve is of course one half, but there are apparently no straight-through reducers in the western universe that go from two to half an inch in one go. Or even in two goes. I wound up using a two to one and a half reducer with a one and a half to one bushing inside that, a one inch bushing into a three-fourths bushing, and a three-foruths to quarter inch bushing. This is then, of course, attached to a male threaded adapter with a one inch length of half inch PVC. Even with all the parts the fittingswould have cost a hair under five bucks.
����The power of the cannon is nothing truly spectacular, in the scheme of things, but it's quite impressive to anyone who hasn't handled a pneumatic cannon pumped to 125 PSI before. It can throw a fifty caliber mustketball a good hundred yards if you angle it right, and can blast a ball of air dry clay clean through five stacked up AOL CD's. Punching holes in things aside, the three-quarter inch barrel can also fire paintballs to insane ranges and lob cratermakers a considerable distance.
����All in all, good clean fun. Plans and digrams will be coming up shortly.

~Zero

Re: Fun with a Tire Valve
The trouble is, though, that the valve doesn't move the pressure from the chamber to the barrel, it just equalizes it. If your barrel is the same volume as the chamber, you start accelerating the load with the same pressure as the chamber, but you're down to half that by the time the projectile leaves the barrel. If you have a 120 PSI chamber and the barrel is twice the volume, you've still got 40 PSI at the end of the barrel.

Re: Fun with a Tire Valve
����If you use a full bore 20 ounce CO2 tank with no regulator the pressure between the chamber of the cannon and the tank would try to equalize. Once it got about a fourth of the way though that, however, the cannon would explode violently.
����You could indeed use a 12 gram, but you would have to work out how much volume you need in your chamber to keep the full charge of the 12 gram at 100-125 PSI. If your chamber were too small you would have the same problem as you would with a larger CO2 tank (though probably not as severe), and if your chamber were too large the cannon would not fire very far.
����The regulator idea works quite nicely, but it is hard to find a CO2 regulator that you can wind all the way down to 100 PSI. I've seen people's cannons that do this, though. When you open the valve the CO2 will continue to flow from the regulator, since the cannon's chamber is effectively at 0 PSI, so you'd have to remember to close the valve after each shot to avoid wasting CO2.

~Zero

Keep in mind that I haven't technically taken a physics class, and I've been up for about 36hrs, but anyway here are my ideas:

The ideal barrel legnth would be whatever equals the volume of the pressure chamber. When the projectile is at the very end of the barrel, the gas in the barrel should equal the gas in the pressure chamber. If the volume of the barrel is less than that of the pressure chamber, not all the gas will be used in accelerating the projectile. If the volume of the barrel is greater than that of the pressure chamber, all the gas will be used in accelerating the projectile, but the projectile will essentially run into more resistance from friction than acceleration from pressure.
