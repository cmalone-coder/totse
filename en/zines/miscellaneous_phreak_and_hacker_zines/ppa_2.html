PROBERS: HACKING IS CRIME OF '90s

By Rory J. O'Connor
and Tom Schmitz
Mercury News Staff Writers

PHOENEX - Warning that the illegal use of computers is becoming "the
white-collar crime of the 1990s," government investigators served notice
Wednsday they are stepping up efforts to thwart people who use their machines
as a liscence to steal.

Secret Service agents joined the Arizona Attorney General and the U.S.
attorney in Phoenex to release partial results of an 18-month investigation
covering 15 cities, including San Jose.

The agents displayed computers, telephone test equipment, and modems --
part of a haul seized in raids across the country that included more than 40
computers and 22,000 computer disks.

Authorities said they expect data stored on the disks will show that
"hackers" with personal computers are engaged in widespread trafficking in
stolen credit cards and long-distance codes -- information that may have been
used to defraud companies and individuals of millions of dollars in goods and
services.

"I can Safely say that this appears to be the largest (investigation)
so far," said Tim Holtzen, assistant U.S. attorney for Arizona. "But this may
well be the tip of the iceberg."

The briefing was the government's first public discussion of "Operation
Sundevil," a joint probe begun in 1988 by the Secret Service and the organized
crime and racketeering division of the Arizona Attorney General's Office.

SEARCH WARRANTS

On Tuesday, 150 Secret Service agents used search warrants to conduct
the seizures. The raids resulted in three arrests, but all were for offenses
unrelated to the investigation, such as illegal possession of a firearm.

While no charges have been filed in any of the cases, authorities said
they suspect the hackers of breaking into numerous computer systems used banks,
private corporations and government agencies. In one case, investigators said,
a hacker managed to alter medical records at an Arizona Hospital.

Officials declined to give any names of victims or estimate losses. But
Stephen McNamee, the U.S. attorney for Arizona, said he knew of one case where
the damage could total $1.7 million.

S.J SEARCH

In San Jose, police detectives assigned to the technology crime unit
conducted a 6 a.m. search of a home in Almaden Valley. Investigators entered
the room of an 18-year old San Jose State University student (Frank, aka Wind
Walker), confiscating a computer system and 800 to 1,000 floppy disks stored
in shoe boxes.

While the student's name apeared Wednesday in several Bay Area media
reports, the Mercury News generally does not identify people involved in
police investigations unless they're charged with committing a crime. The
student could not be reached for comment.

Police said the student was not arrested. However, the disks and
computer equipment were handed over to federal agents for shipment to
Washington, where they will be examined to determine whether the teen-ager is
involved in a fraud ring.

Police said the student apparently operated a computer bulliten board,
which is a computer program that allows users to exchange information over
phone lines. He named his bulliten board the "Billionaire Boys Club." There is
nothing illegal about operating a computer bulliten board unless it is used in
connection with theft or fraud.

The student told police the names of several people who helped operate
the board, said Sgt. Dave Flory, the high-tech crime expert for the San Jose
police.

In addition to San Jose, cities in which search warrants were executed
included Phoenix; Chicago; Cincinnati; Detroit; Los Angeles; Miami; Newark;
N.J.; New York; Pittsburgh; San Diego; Richmond, Va.; and Plano, Texas.

Those under investigation range in age from teen-agers to people in
their Mid-40s, said Steve Purdy, who heads the fraud division of the Secret
Service.

The theft of credit card numbers and telephon access card numbers via
computer has been practiced for years by technically savvy "phone phreaks," who
swap the information through secret computer bulliten boards.

Computer break-ins also have been accomplished by a small number of
so-called hackers, usually teen-age boys who commit the crimes as a prank or an
initiation rite to prove their skill.

However, the investigators said Wednsday that such activity is shifting
to older and more sophisticated hackers.

"Our experience shows that many computer hacker suspects are no longer
misguided teen-agers mischievously playing games with their computers in their
bed-rooms," said Garry M. Jenkins, assistant director of the Secret Service.

It is unclear how the hackers in various cities were connected, and
authorities refused to discuss details of their relationships. But Robert K.
Corbin, attorney general for Arizona, said most were motivated by the thrill
of beating the system, rather than monetary gain.

Gary Chapman, executive director of Computer Proffesionals for Social
Responsibility, applauded the government's effor to send a strong message to
computer criminals. But he said the investigators were overestimating both the
scope of such illegal activity and the damage it has caused.

"Compare computer hacking with the actions of Ivan Boesky or Michael
Milken, and it sort of pales by comparison," Chapman said.

Federal statutes make it a crime to tamper with so-called "federal-
interest computers," which include those used by banks, and other financial
institutions, government agencies, or two or more users in more than one state.

In addition, the law prohibits trafficking in access devices such as
credit card numbers and long-distance codes. Defendants convicted of
unauthorized use of such devices can be sentanced up to 10 years in prison if
they commit fraud of more than $1,000.

TEEN HACKING THREAT DOWNPLAYED
Parents must teach ethics, experts say

By Rory J. O'Connor
Mercury News Computing Editor

In the wake of a nationwide sweep last week aimed at computer crime,
parents must be wondering if that comuter in their kid's bedroom could be
leading their teen-ager down the path to high-tech juvenile delinquency.

Law Enforcement Officials involved in the investigation called computer
crime the white-collar crime of the 1990s and alerted parents that their kids'
fun with computers might have a dark side.

But many programmers and other computer experts say that's an
oversimplification that could cause parents undue concern.

Most serious Computer crime is committed by adults out for personal
profit who have access to corporate computers containing financial information
or sensitive records, the experts say.

By contrast, most teen-age computer enthusiasts will probably never
trouble their parents with anything more dangerous that an unexpectedly large
phone bill.

Federal and Arizone law enforcement officials on Tuesday conducted
searched in 13 cities, including San Jose, as part of their investigation into
the use of computers to trade stolen cred-card numbers and long-distance
telephone codes.

"I think parents need to know that if their son or daughter has a
computer, they may be involved in this," Arizona Attorney State General Robert
K. Corbin said at a news conference Wednesday.

But Gary Chapman, director of Computer Proffesionals for Social
Responsibility in Palo Alto, cautioned: "Most kids who use computers don't
engage in any criminal activity at all. At the fundamental level, if you put a
gun or a car into the hands of a kid, maybe he manages to kill someone by
mistake. It's pretty hard to imagine that being done by someone who's just been
givin a computer."

-ONE IN FIVE ARRESTED IS A TEEN-

According to the National Center for Computer Crime Data in Santa Cruz,
20 percent of those arrested for computer crimes in 1988 were younger that 20,
and about a quarter were students. Nearly half were in their 20s, and 26
percent either worked in a computer-related occupation or had access to a
computer at work. Indeed, 6 percent of the computer criminals in 1988 worked in
Law Enforcement, down from the 28 percent in 1986, according to the center.

The real issue isn't whether access to computers in the bedroom can
turn kids into criminals, but the lack of responsible behavior by some parents,
said Glenn Tenney, a San Mateo programmer and orginizer of the Hackers
Conference, an annual gathering of Highly skilled programmers in Silicon
Valley.

Dealing with teen-age computer use is no different from dealing with
teen-agers driving cars or having sex, Tenney maintains.

EXPERT CAUTIONS PARENTS

While Parents will make sure their teen-agers attend driver's education
classes before taking their car out for a spin, he contends the same approach
isn't always used with computers.

"Some parents may not know right from wrong when it comes to
computers," he said. "They may buy a computer to use at home and take a copy of
the office software to use with it and not stop to think that it may be illegal
or unethical."

Parents whose teen-agers have turned to computer crim are almost always
surprised when police arrive at their homes, armed with search warrants.

Most are like the parents of Kevin Poulson, a 24-year old computr whiz
who has been wanted since January by federal authorities on numerous charges
involving computer and telephone fraud. For a year before Poulson's first brush
with the law in 1983, his father had assumed that Poulson's long stints at the
keybnoard were the harmless tinkerings of an ordinary boy.

"He was on it many hours a day. He would stay up all night with it,"
Lee Poulson said in January. "I didn't really know what he was up to until the
law knocked on our door."

KIDS NEED COMPUTERS

While incidents like this might make parents think twice about buying
computers for their youngsters, law enforcement officials say the solution
isn't keeping computers away from kids.

Doing that, the say, would deprive budding users of the opportunity to
learn skills they'll need to survive in the modern, high-tech adult world.

Nor should they prevent interested youths in starting their own
computer bulletin boards -- computer services that serve as informal
information exchanges among computer users -- but that information could
include stolen credit card and phone card numbers.

Some computer experts like Chapman chastise the government for blowing
the problem of illicit computer use by teens out of proportion.

"Compare all the damage done by computer hacking with the fraud and
abuse in the savings and loan industry," he said. "Computer hacking doesn't
even come within a fraction of a percentage point of that. It's just the
publicity and the attention the media pays to this or that kid playing with a
computer in their bedroom who's supposed to be causing havoc."

Law enforcement officials and computer experts agree, however, that
parents should take notice if their teen-agers spend hours on end using a
computer, especially if they also tie up the phone at the same time.

INTEREST VS. OBSESSION

While it could just spell out the computer-age equivalient of all-night
phone calls to friends, it could also mean the beginnings of an obsession with
