( Our Food Chain is Poisoned and Polluted )

Here on Stone Mountain we grow much of what we eat. The
reason for this is not just for survival but because we are aware
that the commercial food supply is poisoned and to eat from it
has little, if any, benefit. We have learned many tricks to grow
food which is chemical free, high in nutrients and tasty to our
palettes. We thought you might just want to grow wholesome, high
protein, nourishing food for your families and rid yourselves of
many chemicals that are harmful to your evolving light bodies.
Bill Huxel, our neighbor and resident Lemurian organic
gardener would like to share his knowledge with you our growing
family. He has written the following article and is offering
weekend seminars (details below) for the Spring of 1991.

Natural Gardening is an evolution towards an agriculture
which is in harmony with the earth spirits. It requires us to
let go of our own desires and to look at the garden with an eye
toward long-term results. In nature there is no good or bad,
only a movement toward balance. Who are we to call poison ivy




and briars bad? Certainly some plants are more friendly toward
us than others, but there is a reason for why and where different
plants grow. The Mother Earth moves in total acceptance of the
forces that work upon her. Treated with love and respect she
responds with bounty.

In the garden we try to work a change, and do it in ways
that increases the life-force of the space. This spring we will
be starting many different kinds of perennial and annual herbs,
vegetables and flowers from seed. Starting plants from seed is a
simple yet exacting craft that greatly increases both the beauty
and productivity of the garden. Anyone who is serious about
growing their own food and herbs should think about some kind of
simple greenhouse or cold-frame set-up.

We prepare ground in the garden with hand tools, using Alan
Chadwick's double-digging method as a starting point. Almost all
plantings are done in permanent "beds", which gives us the
advantage of focusing soil amendments in the same space year
after year, while leaving the spaces between the beds undis-
turbed, so that natural ground covers such as clovers, grasses
and wild herbs can also thrive in the garden.

Composting is essential to improving the soil and to moving
the garden toward the sustainable closed system it can and should
be. Fertility can only be maintained and increased through
